Why we still cut dials on a 1931 rose engine

A machine older than Indian independence, a lever moved by hand, and 120 passes for a single lehariya dial.

8 November 2026 · 6 minute read

The rose engine at the back of our atelier arrived from a closing workshop in the Jura in 2019, in four crates and with no manual. It is a lathe that rocks: a set of brass cams, called rosettes, push the spindle side to side as it turns, and a cutter held still against the blank traces that movement as a line. Cut one line, index the blank a fraction of a degree, cut the next. A pattern is nothing more than patience, repeated.

Our lehariya dial takes 120 passes. Each wave on the Prahar dial is one pass of the cutter, with the phase shifted by a few hundredths of a turn so the ripples drift outward like dye moving through cloth. A CNC machine could do this in four minutes. It would also do it perfectly, and that is the problem: a hand-cut dial has a living edge, a slight variation of depth from line to line that catches light differently as you turn your wrist.

What the hand gives the light

Under a loupe, a stamped dial shows a pattern pressed into soft metal; the lines are rounded at the bottom. An engine-turned line is a cut, with a flat floor and two crisp walls. The walls are what you see. They work like tiny mirrors, so the dial glows from inside rather than reflecting a single highlight.

“A CNC machine would do it perfectly. That is the problem.”

Mehul, who runs the machine, can cut four dials on a good day. The blanks are solid silver; after cutting they are galvanised in a colour bath, lacquered nine times, and only then printed. If a single pass slips, the dial is melted down and the silver goes back into the next batch.

Why it costs what it costs

About a fifth of the price of a Prahar is its dial. We could halve that. We have decided not to, because the first thing you see when you look at your wrist should be the part that took longest to make.

Written at the bench in Udaipur
