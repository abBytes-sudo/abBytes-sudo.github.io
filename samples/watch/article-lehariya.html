Lehariya, from turban to dial

How a Rajasthani tie-dye became the pattern on our first watch.

30 September 2026 · 4 minute read

Lehariya means wave. Dyers in Jodhpur and Jaipur roll a length of thin cotton diagonally, tie it at intervals and dip it, so the colour runs in ripples when the cloth is opened. It is the pattern of the monsoon turban, worn when the rains arrive.

We spent three months translating that ripple into a rosette for the rose engine. The first version looked like a fingerprint. The fifth looked like water. We kept the fourteen crests per turn because, under the hands at ten past ten, they frame the wordmark like a ripple frames a stone.

The colours

Each dial colour is named after a place: Pichola blue for the lake at night, Sandstone for Jaisalmer at dusk, Monsoon slate for the sky before rain, Aravalli green for the hills after August. Ivory grand feu is the exception, an enamel fired at 820 degrees with no guilloché at all, for people who prefer silence.

Written at the bench in Udaipur
