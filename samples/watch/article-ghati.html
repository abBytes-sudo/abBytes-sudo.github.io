The ghati: India’s twenty-four-minute clock

Before clock towers, temples and courts measured the day with a copper bowl and a bucket of water. We named the maison after it.

21 October 2026 · 5 minute read

A ghati is a small copper bowl with a hole in its base no wider than a hair. Set it floating in a tub of water and it fills slowly, then sinks. The time it takes to sink is one ghatika: twenty-four minutes, a sixtieth of a day and night. A servant would lift it out, empty it, set it floating again, and strike a gong.

For centuries the ghati kept official time across the subcontinent, from temple rituals to the watches of a city’s guards. Astronomical texts describe how to make one: the bowl’s weight, the diameter of the hole, even the gold wire used to drill it so the hole would not corrode wider over time.

“A watch is the same idea, made small: energy released at a steady rate and counted.”

Why the name

We wanted a name that meant time as something made by hand and kept by attention. A ghati only works if someone watches it. A hand-wound watch only works if you wind it. Both are rituals of care, and both reward you with a quiet, exact rhythm.

The bowl appears on every Ghatika caseback, engraved around the edge of the sapphire, and in the monogram on the crown: a circle with a single point at its base.

Written at the bench in Udaipur
